## Funding & M&A

The dining-reservation stack is consolidating rapidly into the hands of three financial giants, and the stakes are the guest relationship itself.

**American Express** is in the process of folding Tock into Resy this summer, sunsetting the Tock brand and creating a 25,000-venue platform. 
The move boosts Resy's venue count to 25,000, formally uniting two onetime startups against reservations market leader OpenTable, the incumbent provider they each hoped to disrupt.
 The financial logic is measurable: 
in the three weeks following the $400 annual Platinum card credit announcement, there was a 36% increase in Resy reservations made by users with a U.S. Platinum card linked to their account — and a five times increase in the daily average number of accounts being linked. People using the Resy credit are spending, on average, 25% more.


**American Express** is now pushing further into Europe. 
The proposed $700 million acquisition of TheFork from Tripadvisor would add its network of more than 50,000 restaurants across 11 European countries to American Express' growing dining ecosystem. Combined with Resy and Tock, the acquisition would expand American Express' network to approximately 75,000 bookable venues worldwide, creating one of the largest restaurant reservation and dining engagement platforms in the industry.
 The deal also frees Tripadvisor to concentrate on its Viator experiences business.

**DoorDash** opened a second front earlier in 2026 with its 
$1.2 billion acquisition of SevenRooms, a reservation platform focused on direct bookings through a restaurant's own website. The company entered the reservations game to capture the full range of dining possibilities, whether delivery, takeout, or table, and is using DashPass membership to offer exclusive tables at trendy spots.
 Now Wall Street is flagging a third front: 
an expected point-of-sale technology rollout from DoorDash is poised to deal a blow to payments firms such as Toast, according to Rothschild & Co Redburn's Dominic Ball, who cut his recommendation on Toast's stock to neutral from buy, saying he expects DoorDash's next expansion to involve in-store restaurant technology.
 The emerging ecosystem logic is documented clearly: 
SevenRooms co-founder Joel Montaniel said "Delivery and dine-in have typically been siloed data sets. So if a customer has ordered six times, and they're coming into the restaurant for the first time, are they a first-time customer or a seventh-time customer?"


**Chowbus** raised $81 million led by Prysm Capital and Left Lane Capital, 
with the company reporting more than $120 million in annual recurring revenue, representing ninefold growth over the past four years, and roughly $4 billion in annualized processed transaction volume.
 
CEO Linxin Wen pointed to the early success of the company's AI Digital Ads product as evidence that AI can help independent restaurants compete more effectively against national chains with larger marketing budgets.


**Nory**, a London-based AI-native restaurant operations platform, 
raised a $37 million Series B led by Kinnevik, bringing total funding to approximately $63 million, and opened a U.S. headquarters in New York. The platform combines workforce management, inventory, procurement, business intelligence, and financial reporting in a single system.
 The company reports customers typically see labor savings of 8–20% and food waste reductions of up to 50%.

On the hotel side, 
hospitality technology startups raised more than $1 billion across 40 companies between April 2025 and March 2026. The biggest raise went to Mews ($300 million), followed by Kindred ($125 million) and Limehome (€75 million), all within a single 90-day window.
 
PMS was the clear funding leader at $408.1 million — more than any other category — because PMS is increasingly becoming the control layer of the hospitality tech stack. Mews' acquisitions of Flexkeeping and DataChat in late 2025 show the best-funded PMS businesses using that position to build broader platforms through acquisition rather than third-party integrations.


Sources: [Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/91496951/amex-resy-tock-restaurant-reservation-wars) | [Restaurant Technology News – TheFork](https://restauranttechnologynews.com/2026/06/american-express-expands-restaurant-technology-ambitions-with-700-million-thefork-acquisition/) | [Hotel Technology News – $1B Index](https://hoteltechnologynews.com/2026/04/research-hospitality-technology-tops-1-billion-across-40-startups-with-pms-and-ai-platforms-capturing-the-largest-share/)

So what: The reservation stack is now owned by three financial services giants (Amex, DoorDash/delivery, Chase/OpenTable), not restaurant tech companies. For mid-market operators, the leverage dynamic has shifted: platforms are paying operators to switch. For PE-backed hospitality SaaS, the existential question is where your platform sits relative to these ecosystems — partner, acquisition target, or irrelevant.

## AI Visibility & Discovery

The infrastructure layer governing how AI agents find and book hospitality properties is being built now, and the choices being made this week will set distribution norms for a decade.

**Google** expanded AI Mode's agentic restaurant booking to eight new international markets in April. 
Users can describe preferences in natural language — party size, cuisine, ambiance — and AI Mode searches multiple reservation platforms to surface bookable options. Australia, Canada, Hong Kong, India, New Zealand, Singapore, South Africa, and the UK make up the eight new markets. Google pointed to UK searches for "when to book a table" surging 140% in 2026 as context for the timing.
 The reservation partners powering the feature include OpenTable, TheFork, SevenRooms, and five regional platforms. 
Google simultaneously built out AI Mode's monetization layer, with Direct Offers ad formats, Universal Commerce Protocol checkout flows, and in-March confirmation from SVP Nick Fox that ads were actively being tested — Google's VP of Ads describing the strategy as "reinventing what an ad is."


For hotels, Google has named the sector as the next UCP vertical. 
Google announced that hotel booking will be the next focus for its Universal Commerce Protocol, with the Agent Payments Protocol enabling AI agents to complete purchases on users' behalf starting with Gemini.
 Critically, 
Google and OpenAI are diverging strategically: while Google is expanding direct transaction capabilities inside its AI ecosystem, OpenAI has reportedly stepped back from enabling direct purchases inside ChatGPT.


**Amadeus** announced this week at HITEC 2026 that it is Google's only B2B hospitality partner on UCP. 
Amadeus has two product announcements — AI Commerce, which makes hotels bookable through AI assistant channels, and the official launch of Amadeus Max, which lets hotel staff query data in plain English. But the bigger story is infrastructure: if UCP becomes the standard, Amadeus will have helped write the rules. Hotels that can't connect to agentic booking channels at speed and scale risk something worse than losing bookings to competitors — they risk not surfacing at all.


The traveler data is nuanced. 
AI travel search is growing 50% faster than traditional search and 43% of travelers already use AI when planning a trip. However, 53% of travelers are comfortable letting AI suggest a hotel, but 66% say they would not trust AI to book on their behalf — meaning AI drives discovery, not necessarily the booking itself.
 Expedia Group's own data puts the share of travelers actively relying on ChatGPT or Gemini for trip planning at just 8%.

Sources: [WinBuzzer – Google 8 Countries](https://winbuzzer.com/2026/04/13/google-ai-mode-rolls-out-restaurant-booking-to-8-countries-xcxwbn/) | [Skift Daily – Amadeus HITEC](https://newsletters.skift.com/p/ai-tools-target-hotel-booking) | [Lighthouse / HospitalityNet – AI Visibility Guide](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/explainer/4132306/the-independent-hoteliers-guide-to-ai-visibility-in-2026)

So what: Hotels and restaurants are entering an era where machine readability — structured data, clean APIs, consistent content across channels — is a more important distribution lever than paid search spend. Independent operators who clean up their data layer now are building a moat that ad budget cannot replicate. The Amadeus/Google UCP deal means that for hotels, your CRS vendor's UCP compatibility is a buying criterion, not a footnote.

## Vendor Moves

Platform consolidation from opposite directions defines this cycle's most important competitive dynamics.

**Toast** launched Toast Drive-Thru in April, 
an enterprise-grade solution designed to help QSRs increase throughput and order accuracy at scale across 140,000+ U.S. drive-thru locations. The platform integrates POS, kitchen display systems, digital menu boards, and AI voice ordering partnerships with companies including Incept AI, along with Speed of Service reporting to identify lane bottlenecks.
 Toast CMO Kelly Esten said at the National Restaurant Association Show that restaurant technology has to emphasize reliability.

**DoorDash** updated its Preferred Integration Partner programme with tightened standards. 
The 2026 roster comprises Checkmate, Deliverect, Otter, Square, Stream, Toast and UrbanPiper. The updates build on tougher integration standards and new tools aimed at improving order accuracy, reducing cancellations, and simplifying day-to-day operations — while the broader analyst community is now watching for DoorDash's expected move into in-store POS.


**Oracle/NetSuite** launched Restaurant Operations in March, 
unifying inventory, procurement, scheduling, production, and cash management data in a single AI-enhanced platform, consolidating data from Oracle Simphony Cloud and other POS systems for a single source of truth across locations.


**Booking Holdings** reported hard metrics at its April 28 earnings call. 
CFO Ewout Steenbergen said customer service costs are down about 10% per reservation while bookings were up roughly 10%, calling the results "tangible." Priceline's AI agent "Penny" is producing higher engagement in early tests; OpenTable's AI Concierge initially answered 80% of diner questions at its July 2025 launch; and Agoda reported a double-digit year-over-year reduction in customer service cost per booking.


**Cendyn** launched Wayfinder, described as a hotel AI visibility monitoring tool showing operators how their property appears across AI search platforms. (No operator outcome data yet — see Agentwashing table.)

**Lighthouse** launched Ernest, described as an AI agent helping hotel commercial teams turn data insights into revenue actions. (No operator outcome data in the ticker announcement.)

Sources: [Toast Drive-Thru PR](https://pos.toasttab.com/news/toast-drive-thru-launch-2026) | [Bloomberg – DoorDash POS Threat](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-05-12/doordash-poses-disruption-risk-to-payments-firms-analyst-says) | [Let's Data Science – Booking Holdings AI](https://letsdatascience.com/news/booking-holdings-expands-ai-assistants-cuts-service-costs-2e909e40)

So what: Two ecosystems — DoorDash (demand-native) and Toast (operations-native) — are racing toward the same "restaurant operating system" destination. Operators choosing between them are making a strategic bet on whether data about the customer (DoorDash) or data about the kitchen (Toast) provides the stronger commercial moat. For PE-backed hospitality SaaS, either platform acquiring you or locking you out of preferred integration lists is now a board-level scenario to model.

## Data & Integration

The central competitive question in hospitality tech has shifted from feature velocity to data architecture.

The integration question has been reformulated: 
it used to be "Can your PMS talk to system X?" Now it's "Can an AI agent read across all your integrated systems and act on behalf of the front desk?" AI receptionists now answer guest questions by querying PMS, CRM, and POS data simultaneously. Conversational AI handles reservation changes that write back to the PMS automatically. Agentic upsell engines read guest history and reservation context to surface relevant offers. None of this works without a clean integration layer underneath.


In the restaurant sector, the same problem is articulated with sharper commercial urgency. 
Saleem Khatri, CEO of Lavu, wrote: "A generic AI wrapper is not proprietary intelligence. A general-purpose language model doesn't know what a void spike at 3 PM on Bar 2 means, or why overtime being negatively correlated with revenue at half your locations should alarm you. Ask directly: 'What data did you train on? What happens to my product if OpenAI raises prices 10x tomorrow?' If the answer involves the phrase 'we leverage cutting-edge LLM technology,' they're reselling someone else's product at a markup."


For restaurants, the back-office integration gap remains acute. 
AI layered on top of fragmented systems rarely changes performance. Integration — not novelty — determines whether AI becomes decoration or execution. Menus must function as structured data layers: machine-readable, standardized in naming and modifiers, synchronized with POS systems, automatically updated across platforms, and accessible via API.


Sources: [HotelSmarters – PMS Integration](https://hotelsmarters.com/blog/hotel-pms-integration) | [RTN – How to Tell if Your Restaurant AI Is Real](https://restauranttechnologynews.com/2026/04/how-to-tell-if-your-restaurant-ai-is-real/) | [QSR Magazine – ROI failures](https://www.qsrmagazine.com/story/why-most-restaurant-ai-investments-fail-to-deliver-roi/)

So what: Before buying another AI product, audit your data plumbing. If your PMS, CRS, and CRM can't serve a clean guest record in under ten seconds, or if your restaurant's menu isn't machine-readable, you're building on sand. This is unglamorous infrastructure work, but it's the only work that makes the next generation of agentic tools actually function.

## Governance & Security

The legal and regulatory environment around hospitality AI hardened significantly in early June.


On June 2, 2026, the White House issued an Executive Order titled "Promoting Advanced Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Security." On its face, the Order is about federal systems and national cyber defense, but it is one of the clearest signals that artificial intelligence has crossed a threshold — from an experimental productivity tool to a critical layer of operational infrastructure that governments now treat as a matter of national security.


For hotel operators, Terence Ronson of Pertlink Limited translated the EO into operational priorities: 
AI capability is now treated as national strategic capability, with the Order creating a classified benchmarking process to designate "covered frontier models" and a voluntary framework giving the government up to 30 days' early access. Notably, the Order explicitly states it does not impose any mandatory licensing or permitting requirements for developing or releasing AI models — meaning accountability accrues to organizations disciplined enough to govern themselves.


On the European side, a hard deadline is approaching: 
the EU AI Act Article 50 transparency requirements for chatbots apply from August 2, 2026. High-risk AI obligations apply from August 2026 to Annex III systems, with some phased obligations following in August 2027. The FTC's Unfair or Deceptive Fees Rule covers short-term lodging, and California's Automated Decisionmaking Technology access and opt-out rights take effect January 1, 2027.


For hospitality operators handling bulk customer data, U.S. DOJ rules under Executive Order 14117 present a new exposure: 
the DOJ Data Security Program restricts certain transactions that could give countries of concern access to bulk sensitive personal data. Hospitality companies routinely touch at least four of the covered sensitive data categories — identifiers, geolocation, biometrics, and financial — and AI deployments amplify this exposure by aggregating and reusing data for training and inference across systems.


The governance-containment gap is the defining security challenge. 
63% of organizations cannot enforce purpose limitations on AI agents, 60% cannot terminate misbehaving agents quickly, and 55% cannot isolate AI systems from sensitive networks — a 15-20 point gap between watching and acting. Organizations without evidence-quality audit trails trail by 20-32 points on every AI governance metric measured.


Sources: [Hospitalitynet – White House AI EO](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/opinion/4132771/when-national-ai-security-becomes-a-hospitality-imperative) | [Witness.ai – Hospitality AI Security](https://witness.ai/blog/conversational-ai-hospitality/) | [Hospitality Technology – DOJ Bulk Data](https://hospitalitytech.com/beyond-hipaa-navigating-dojs-new-bulk-data-thresholds-hospitality-ai)

So what: The August 2, 2026 EU AI Act chatbot transparency deadline is less than seven weeks away. Any hotel or restaurant group running AI-facing guest communications in Europe that hasn't mapped those systems to Article 50 requirements is now in a compliance sprint, not a planning phase. The DOJ bulk data rules present a second, quieter exposure for any operator using offshore AI vendors that touch loyalty, biometric, geolocation, or payment data.

## Operational AI

The restaurant industry's AI reality check is the sharpest signal in this briefing cycle.

The National Restaurant Association's state-of-industry report in February found that 
26% of restaurant operators say they are using AI-related tools
, but at the NRA Show in May, 
restaurant tech leaders almost universally said the industry is getting AI wrong, even amid enthusiasm on the show floor. Square's head of food and beverage called it "the biggest thing of this entire show." A counterpoint came from a skeptic who argued that overadoption of large language models rests on shaky fundamentals — the cost of AI tokens required to operate coding services can exceed the salaries of the workers the technology purports to replace.


The AI-washing argument from Lavu CEO Khatri provides the sharpest operator-facing framework of this cycle: 
the restaurant industry has an AI-washing problem. Vendors are slapping "AI-powered" onto tools that haven't materially changed in years, and the hype cycle is producing more confusion than value.
 His test: 
if an AI product increases your monthly software spend, it must increase your operating margin by more than it costs.


For hotels, Booking Holdings provides the clearest proof-of-ROI benchmark in this cycle — with customer service costs down 10% per reservation even as bookings grew 10%, alongside OpenTable's AI Concierge answering 80% of diner questions at launch. These are the numbers operators should be asking vendors to match.
