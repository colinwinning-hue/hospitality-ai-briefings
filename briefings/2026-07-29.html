Vendor Moves — The Reservation Wars Reshape Restaurant Data Ownership

The most consequential structural shift in restaurant technology this cycle is not a product launch: it is a three-way battle for ownership of the diner's identity and reservation history, with data rather than tables as the real prize.

DoorDash / SevenRooms delivered the most substantive product news of the week. On July 21, SevenRooms announced the Global Guest capability for partner restaurants to search hundreds of millions of DoorDash and Deliveroo customers — formally introduced just over a year after DoorDash completed its $1.2 billion acquisition of the CRM platform. The practical implication: the feature enables restaurant staff to recognize guests at check-in, even for first-time visitors, collapsing the historical firewall between off-premise delivery data and on-premise hospitality. The slate of tools also included one that surfaces targeted incentives, like complimentary cocktails, within booking flows to fill off-peak time slots, plus AI-powered automatic tightening and loosening of table capacity depending on traffic. Separately, SevenRooms launched Channel Connect in June — a free, open-licence reservations aggregator that pulls bookings from major platforms including OpenTable and Resy into a single dashboard, offering "more tables for diners, one book for restaurants," in the words of CEO Joel Montaniel.

The move comes amid fierce competition: in April, OpenTable began requiring restaurant clients to make it their system of record; Channel Connect is at least partially a direct response, with SevenRooms stating "no single platform has to be the gatekeeper to your operations."

American Express is building the other superpower in the space. This summer Amex is merging Tock's restaurant inventory with Resy's platform, roughly doubling Resy's library to more than 25,000 venues. The move sunsets the Tock brand, formally uniting two onetime OpenTable rivals against the market leader.

The proposed $700 million acquisition of TheFork from Tripadvisor — announced June 15 and expected to close before year-end — would add more than 50,000 restaurants across 11 European countries, expanding the combined Amex dining network to approximately 75,000 bookable venues. Amex CEO of Resy Pablo Rivero has framed the ambition clearly: to build a powerful tech stack linking AI-powered discovery and booking, table and guestbook management, content and curation, ticketing and transactions, check-level POS data, and American Express membership. The commercial flywheel is already measurable: in the three weeks following the Platinum card dining credit announcement, Resy saw a 36% increase in reservations made by Platinum cardholders — and people using the credit are spending, on average, 25% more.

Toast / AmEx partnership closes a critical loop in the stack. The companies plan to build tools combining Resy and Tock's guestbook capabilities with Toast's Digital Chits technology — which allows restaurant staff to view important customer information directly on Toast Go handhelds and POS terminals during service. The partnership also intends to make Resy and Tock listings available on the Local by Toast app. Toast meanwhile published Q1 2026 data showing that operators at more than 125,000 restaurants started millions of threads with Toast IQ — the operational AI assistant launched in October 2025. Most common questions were about sales and revenue (47%), followed by menu and inventory (34%) and guests and marketing (32%); forecasting and planning registered just 1%. Fine-dining operators used it most heavily: fine-dining locations had 29% more threads with Toast IQ than fast casuals.

Sources: Food On Demand, Nation's Restaurant News — Channel Connect, American Express newsroom, Forbes — Pablo Rivero, Toast newsroom, NRN — Toast IQ

So what: For a restaurant operator, the reservation landscape now has three distinct data strategies behind it. DoorDash/SevenRooms brings delivery graph data into the dining room; AmEx brings spend and loyalty data; Toast brings POS and operational data. The operators who win are those who own the guest profile across all three touchpoints — and the operators most exposed are those who passively let platforms accumulate data rights they haven't read the terms of.

AI Visibility & Discovery — Google Goes Transactional, OpenAI Steps Back

The hotel and restaurant distribution stack is undergoing a structural rewrite as AI platforms shift from discovery tools to transaction layers — at different speeds.

Google used its July 28 earnings call to make travel the centrepiece of its AI monetisation story. The Direct Offers pilot is expanding to include IHG Hotels & Resorts, joining earlier partners Booking Holdings and Expedia, while Google builds toward in-conversation booking through the Universal Commerce Protocol — though key details on checkout, routing, and metrics remain undisclosed. Travel companies are spreading their bets across AI platforms, with IHG also distributing inventory via ChatGPT and Booking powering agentic reservations on OpenTable. The deeper architecture, detailed at Google I/O in May, is significant: Google is stepping out of its role as a search engine that routes users to partners and repositioning as a direct transaction layer. Its hospitality-specific implementation, UCP for Lodging, enables any AI interaction to be converted into an instant room reservation executed directly within Google surfaces — crucially, with the hotel retaining Merchant of Record status and full ownership of the guest relationship. The monetisation question remains unanswered: both Google and LLM platforms are expected to charge affiliate fees on completed transactions, with existing OTA relationships not being severed — though one can reasonably assume a degree of friction.

OpenAI has taken the opposite turn. OpenAI shifted its shopping strategy in March to focus on discovery and research, leaving checkout to third-party app developers. The move alleviated competitive pressure on Booking Holdings and Expedia, whose shares rose on the announcement, and reflects the complexity of handling payment, customer service, and fulfilment for travel bookings at scale. However, the company's enterprise ambitions continue: OpenAI's launch of the OpenAI Deployment Company, including its acquisition of Tomoro — the team behind Virgin Atlantic's AI concierge — signals that AI adoption is increasingly becoming a long-term platform and ecosystem decision rather than a matter of selecting isolated software tools.

Square delivered the most concrete agentic commerce example in restaurants this cycle. On July 1, Square launched an integration letting restaurants accept orders placed directly through ChatGPT and Claude, alongside a companion Alexa+ voice-ordering feature, with no setup required for eligible sellers and no added marketplace commission beyond Square's standard processing fee (~2.9% + 30 cents). Orders route through Square's existing 'Order by Cash App' infrastructure directly into point-of-sale and kitchen-display systems.

The open question is whether consumers actually complete food orders inside a chat interface versus just using AI to decide where to order from and then finishing the transaction elsewhere — that is the actual bet Square is making.

Sources: Skift — Google/IHG earnings, Hospitality ON — Google I/O UCP, Skift — OpenAI walkback, Value Add Pulse — Square

So what: Hotels that have invested in structured data, MCP connectors, and machine-readable rate availability are about to discover whether that work pays off in Google's Direct Offers program. Those who haven't are invisible to an agent completing a booking. Restaurants on Square get agentic commerce for free; watch whether order volume materialises to validate the model.

Operational AI — Voice Ordering Crosses into Infrastructure

Voice AI in restaurant operations moved from experiment to integration layer this week, with the most significant product announcement being the Deliverect-SoundHound partnership.

Deliverect and SoundHound AI announced on July 23 a partnership to create a fully automated ordering channel. Deliverect, serving more than 80,000 restaurant locations worldwide, partnered with SoundHound AI to fully automate restaurant voice ordering from the moment a customer speaks to the moment the kitchen receives the order. The integration connects SoundHound Smart Ordering directly to Deliverect's order and menu management platform, with voice AI agents pulling real-time menu and location data from Deliverect, capturing orders across every voice channel, and routing them straight to kitchen fulfilment through Deliverect's certified POS integrations — no manual re-entry, no handoff errors. The scale claim: with more than 550 POS integrations across over 50 countries and support for more than 100 languages, the partnership gives international restaurant groups ordering infrastructure that scales across channels, markets, and languages without rebuilding for every new touchpoint. SoundHound VP Mike Lauricella: "The future of restaurant hospitality relies on automation and convenience. By syncing critical menu and location data, our AI voice agents can handle order placement flawlessly, reducing front-of-house strain and delivering a seamless customer experience."

The NRA Show 2026 (May, reviewed July 23) surfaced the operator mindset around AI: sceptical of hype, hungry for integration. The Industry Group editor Craig Keefner said: "The winners in 2026 will not be the companies with the loudest AI messaging — they will be the vendors that reduce friction, integrate cleanly with existing POS and payment ecosystems, improve labor efficiency, and still function reliably five years from now." The show's dominant theme: operators are abandoning fragmented, single-point solutions in favour of a consolidated platform architecture, prioritising long-term vendor stability and deep API integration over low-friction, budget-first point tools.

Sources: PRNewswire via Yahoo Finance — Deliverect/SoundHound, Kiosk Industry — NRA 2026 review

So what: The Deliverect-SoundHound deal is the most operationally credible voice AI announcement of this cycle: it addresses the POS fragmentation problem that has killed most voice pilots internationally, and it does so via an existing digital-ordering infrastructure layer rather than a greenfield deployment. Mid-market chains with Deliverect already in stack have a frictionless path to voice ordering across every channel.

Funding & M&A — $1 Billion Hospitality Tech Investment and the PMS Control Layer

Abode Worldwide's Hospitality Tech Investment Index 2026 confirmed that forty global hospitality technology startups raised more than $1 billion between April 2025 and March 2026, with PMS and AI-led platforms attracting the most investment. The three largest raises were Mews at $300 million, home-swapping platform Kindred at $125 million, and Limehome at €75 million. The structural story is in the category breakdown: seven PMS companies raised a combined $408.1 million — more than any other category — because "PMS is increasingly becoming the control layer of the hospitality tech stack," per the report.

Four AI-powered guest experience platforms raised a combined $152.6 million: Duve, Chatlyn, Conduit, and Canary Technologies. Canary notably acquired OpenKey in February 2026 to expand its access to door lock providers. The funding report also noted that 71% of hospitality professionals say AI is having a significant or transformative impact on their industry, and 85% expect to allocate at least 5% of their IT budget to AI tools this year.

Mews used its Unfold 2026 conference to announce what it calls a complete operating system for hospitality. The platform consolidates revenue management, distribution, guest messaging, automations, and financial processing into a single data model. Independent research by IDC puts the business case at 8.7% average revenue growth, €350,000 annual benefit per 100 rooms, a 476% three-year ROI, and a four-month payback period.

Sources: Hotel Dive — $1B funding, Abode Worldwide Investment Index

So what: For PE-backed hospitality SaaS: the market is rewarding PMS control-layer platforms, not point solutions. If you sell a best-of-breed tool without deep PMS integration, you are increasingly competing for a shrinking budget allocation as operators consolidate to fewer, stickier platforms. The compounding advantage of unified data (better AI → higher switching costs → more data → better AI) is now the explicit investment thesis.

AI Visibility & Discovery — Hotels and the OTA Moat Problem

Booking Holdings CEO Glenn Fogel was direct at a May conference: "Google knows everything about you. They don't know what I can offer to you because you are a Genius Level 3." That framing captures the OTA competitive moat in an agent-driven world: loyalty and personalisation data that AI platforms cannot replicate from public signals. But the moat may not hold. AI-referred travelers who arrived with high intent converted 28% less than other visitors weren't failing to book — they were completing their journey through a conversion layer that routes to whoever holds the strongest position in it. For most independent properties, that's an OTA. A traveler discovered through ChatGPT, verified through Google, and landed on an OTA listing has their booking captured by infrastructure the hotel didn't pay for.

Booking Holdings CEO Fogel confirmed to analysts on April 28 that the company is working closely with OpenAI and Google explicitly to ensure Booking's supply is discoverable.

Sources: Booking Holdings / PhocusWire Q1 2026 earnings, hospitality.today — OTA moat analysis

So what: Hotels investing in AI eligibility (structured data, MCP connectors, machine-readable content) without also building a direct conversion pathway are spending on top-of-funnel discovery that OTAs will harvest at the bottom. The question is whether Google's UCP/Lodging model — where the hotel retains Merchant of Record status — changes that dynamic materially. The answer is not yet known.

Governance & Security — The White House EO Lands in Hospitality Compliance

The June 2, 2026 White House Executive Order on AI Innovation and Security is not a hospitality regulation — but a detailed analysis by Pertlink's Terence Ronson translates its provisions into concrete implications for hotel operators. The Order recognises that artificial intelligence has crossed a threshold — from an experimental productivity tool to a critical layer of operational infrastructure that governments now treat as a matter of national security. For hospitality, a modern hotel has quietly become a dense, interconnected intelligent environment binding together identity, payments, guest data, building systems, IoT, and AI-driven workflows. The near-term practical pressure: the Order tasks Treasury with forming an AI cybersecurity clearinghouse, a voluntary collaboration to coordinate vulnerability scanning, validation, and patch distribution across infrastructure operators. Ronson's read: "AI capability is now treated as national strategic capability. For hospitality, the competitive question is shifting from 'How many AI features do we have?' to 'Can we govern AI responsibly, secure our connected systems, and turn operational trust into guest experience?'"

Separately, a regulatory threat with sharper teeth: in February 2026, the UK CMA opened an investigation against three hotel chains and a shared pricing analytics tool suspected of exchanging competitively sensitive information through the same algorithm — a so-called "hub-and-spoke" pattern, following the same logic as the RealPage litigation in the US.

The EU AI Act adds another layer: EU AI Act Article 50 transparency requirements for chatbots apply from 2 August 2026. As AI expands from chatbots to reservations, payments, and service workflows, operators need to map AI deployments to applicable frameworks across jurisdictions.

Sources: Hospitality Net — White House EO analysis, Rield — UK CMA pricing investigation

So what: Revenue management teams sharing the same third-party pricing tool as direct competitors now face regulatory risk that is no longer theoretical. EU chatbot transparency requirements began accruing liability this month. Hotel operators need an AI governance map — not a strategy deck — before year-end.
