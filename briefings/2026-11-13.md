# Hospitality AI Briefing — 2026-11-13

## Data & Integration

The settlement layer for AI-booked hotel rooms acquired its most concrete infrastructure fix of the year. **Duffel** and **Katanox** (11 Nov) announced a partnership designed to route hotel bookings, payments, and commission settlement through fewer intermediaries, with the hotel staying merchant of record throughout. 
Duffel — the travel tech infrastructure company that lets businesses embed flight, hotel, and travel-extra bookings directly into their own apps, recently powering travel for Robinhood and Meta's AI agent Muse — spans 1.6 million hotel properties worldwide. The aim of the partnership is to fix a chain that leaves travelers stranded at check-in and sellers chasing commissions months after checkout.
 
Katanox will connect directly to hotels' property management or central reservation systems, bypassing bed banks and distribution switches. Virtual credit cards would be replaced by bank transfers for hotel settlement, addressing two problems Duffel CEO Steve Domin described: travelers arriving at properties where the hotel cannot find the booking, and commissions that take months to collect. The setup is built with AI booking agents in mind: Katanox CEO Paul Beukers said safeguards could let a platform cancel erroneous reservations or withhold funds before a hotel is paid.
 One structural flag: Domin expects instant or at-checkout settlement, while Beukers described escrow released at check-in when a reservation becomes non-refundable — two materially different products.

**Cloudbeds** confirmed, via a Forbes analysis published 11 November, that 
participating U.S. hotels will appear in Muse with live rates and availability from the first quarter of 2027, with reservations completed through their own booking engines — a direct-booking route into Muse for independent hotels accustomed to paying OTA commissions.


Sources: [Skift, 11 Nov](https://skift.com/2026/11/11/duffel-katanox-partnership-targets-ai-agents-hotel-booking-breakdowns/); [Forbes, 11 Nov](https://www.forbes.com/sites/bernardmarr/2026/11/11/the-biggest-hotel-and-hospitality-technology-trends-for-2027/)

So what: Duffel-Katanox is the most actionable mechanism to date for independent and mid-market hotels to retain merchant-of-record status while AI agents do the booking. For PE-backed hospitality groups assessing 2027 distribution economics, model both timelines now — the difference between instant settlement and escrow-at-check-in is not a rounding error on a leveraged asset.

## Governance & Security

A public dispute over whether hotels should ban AI booking agents opened at the Skift Commerce Summit in New York on 11 November, exposing the conflict between upsell economics and distribution reality.


MCR's Tyler Morse said he wants to ban AI agents from booking his hotels. His reason is that agents "make more rational decisions" and don't buy on impulse, so they are bad for upsell. "Everyone is going to ban the agents," he said, and then admitted: "I can't figure out how to do it." United Airlines announced its own ban on AI bots the same day.



Terence Ronson (Pertlink), responding in a 12 November analysis, argued no hotel can ban its way out of distribution. If the front door is shut, the agent goes round the side: in Singapore and Japan, Agoda is already bookable through Meta's Muse. If Meta then takes a cut on top of the OTA's, a hotel could pay twice for the same room. What hotels need is a bouncer — a policy that says which agents get in, through which door, and on what terms. Skift checked 50 hotel brand websites and found only 10 that address AI crawlers in their robots.txt files.



Practical steps Ronson recommends this week: write an agent access policy (not a ban); put agent liability in writing, turning Citi's principle — that liability for an agent's mistake should fall on "the company that is actually coming with the agent" — into a contract clause; model double commission in the 2027 budget; and rebuild upsell for a rational buyer by making offers structured and rule-based so an agent can accept them for the guest.
 Agoda's 2027 Asia Travel Pulse, surveying 3,395 travellers across 11 markets, found 53% now plan trips with AI, but only 7% would let an agent book and just 6% fully trust an agent to book autonomously — data Ronson reads as a deadline, not permission to wait.

The cybersecurity dimension of agentic commerce also drew fresh attention in a Forbes 11 November piece. 
VikingCloud research found 82% of hotels surveyed experienced a cyberattack during a single summer. Once software can book and pay on a guest's behalf, hotels need to establish which agents are legitimate, what the guest authorized, and how to resolve a disputed transaction — and a compromised agent is not only a payment risk but a physical safety risk, as it could in principle request credentials that open a hotel room door.


Sources: [Hotel News Resource / Pertlink, 12 Nov](https://www.hotelnewsresource.com/article143746.html); [Forbes, 11 Nov](https://www.forbes.com/sites/bernardmarr/2026/11/11/the-biggest-hotel-and-hospitality-technology-trends-for-2027/)

So what: "Everyone is going to ban the agents" is not a strategy — it is an OTA statement waiting to happen. Mid-market operators should audit robots.txt, draft an agent access policy, and add agent-liability language to new vendor and OTA contracts this month. The 7% of travellers willing to let an agent book is a leading indicator that will compound faster than most revenue models currently assume.

## AI Visibility & Discovery

The question of which ordering channel captures restaurant revenue when AI places the order received detailed global mapping in a 12 November Food on Demand analysis. Current agent volumes remain negligible but the race to wire the channel is live.


AI assistants already place orders through delivery marketplaces and through a restaurant's own ordering system. Which route they take determines who earns the commission, who controls the customer relationship, and what is left for the restaurant.



During DoorDash's Q3 earnings call, CEO Tony Xu revealed that out of nearly 1 billion total orders in the quarter, third-party AI agent-placed orders accounted for just a few hundred. Uber Eats did slightly better, with CFO Balaji Krishnamurthy reporting around 500 agentic orders out of its 320 million total food and ride orders during Halloween week.



Noah Glass, CEO of Olo, argued that restaurants need to establish their own connections to AI assistants now, before consumer habits become entrenched — because AI companies and delivery platforms are striking deals at the same time. By the time AI ordering becomes commonplace, where agents send orders may already have been decided. If a marketplace is the only service connected to an assistant, there is no choice. Olo is building connections intended to let restaurants receive orders placed directly through AI assistants while retaining the customer relationship and associated data.



Since July, Square has allowed restaurants to accept orders through ChatGPT and Claude without marketplace commissions, although standard processing fees still apply.
 Internationally, the article documents: Swiggy in India enabling MCP-based ordering through ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini with Razorpay payment integration; KFC China operating its own agent inside Alibaba's Qwen app covering more than 13,000 restaurants; iFood in Brazil running voice ordering through WhatsApp; and Just Eat Takeaway extending voice ordering across eight European countries.

Sources: [Food on Demand, 12 Nov](https://foodondemand.com/11122026/who-takes-the-order-when-ai-orders-lunch/); [Forbes, 11 Nov](https://www.forbes.com/sites/bernardmarr/2026/11/11/the-biggest-hotel-and-hospitality-technology-trends-for-2027/)

So what: The ordering channel is being wired while volumes are still negligible. Restaurants and restaurant tech providers that build direct MCP or API connections now — through Square, Olo, or proprietary routes — position themselves inside AI assistants before marketplace exclusivity hardens. The window to avoid replicating the OTA-commission trap in delivery is open. Swiggy and KFC China demonstrate that it closes quickly once majors move.
